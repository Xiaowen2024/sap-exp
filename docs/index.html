SAP/Warp / Benchmarking / Cross-simulator study

Research report · September 2026

SAP/Warp vs MuJoCo:
contact-rich simulation benchmark

A controlled comparison of contact parameterization, friction, dynamic impact, collision representation, and manipulation robustness.

Study scope
Rigid-body robotics simulation
Experiment set
650+ controlled cross-simulator runs
Comparison basis
Physical observables and failure modes
Research question. Which physical regimes does each formulation reproduce reliably, how transferable are its parameters, and what failure modes appear when contact becomes stiff, dynamic, geometrically complex, or embedded in a manipulation task?

1. Executive Summary

Bottom line

SAP/Warp and MuJoCo show complementary strengths rather than a consistent accuracy ordering.

SAP/Warp exposes more material-like contact parameters and, in the tested compliant-contact regime, follows ideal Coulomb friction more directly near the sticking/sliding boundary.

MuJoCo is more robust in near-rigid, high-stiffness multi-contact and provides stronger native controls for suppressing frictional drift.

Dynamic impact currently favors MuJoCo when comparing the same absolute timestep, but the two engines exhibit substantially different contact timescales; this difference must be controlled before attributing the result purely to numerical integration.

In manipulation, low-level contact advantages do not translate directly into task-level dominance. In the corrected Panda benchmark, SAP/Warp has a substantially larger observed mass/friction feasible region and lower long-hold drift, while MuJoCo is more robust to pose perturbations and exhibits tighter short-horizon object retention in successful grasps.

Across the study, collision representation and contact-manifold behavior often matter as much as the nominal contact formulation itself.

2. Main Findings

2.1 Contact parameter semantics differ fundamentally

SAP/Warp exposes a pair stiffness k_e with a relatively direct material-like interpretation in stable compliant-contact regimes:

Fn ≈ ke δ

MuJoCo's contact stiffness is part of a constraint-dynamics formulation and depends on quantities such as effective mass and impedance. Therefore, the same solref configuration does not automatically correspond to the same physical stiffness when contact topology changes.

This was visible when transferring a calibration from body–plane to dynamic body–body contact: MuJoCo required an effective-mass correction, whereas SAP/Warp's low-stiffness pair law transferred more naturally.

However, this advantage does not persist indefinitely as stiffness increases. At high stiffness, SAP/Warp developed contact switching and vertical jitter in multi-contact stacks, while MuJoCo remained stable. A repeatable approximately 0.1 mm offset between SAP/Warp's constraint coordinate and independently measured geometric overlap was observed in both layered-box and simple-pinch experiments.

Takeaway. SAP/Warp provides more interpretable compliant-contact stiffness semantics, while MuJoCo is currently more robust in the tested near-rigid, high-stiffness regime.

3. Friction

3.1 Sub-Coulomb sticking and creep

Under ideal Coulomb friction,

|Ft| < μFn

should permit zero-velocity sticking.

In the matched soft-friction baseline, SAP/Warp exhibited much less pre-slip and steady drift than MuJoCo.

However, a dedicated MuJoCo anti-slip ablation showed that this is not an engine-level limitation. MuJoCo's creep strongly depends on:

  • impratio
  • solreffriction
  • NoSlip post-processing
  • Contact-manifold and solver settings

Increasing impratio reduced baseline creep by roughly one order of magnitude for each decade increase in the tested regime, while NoSlip could suppress residual drift by several additional orders of magnitude.

Takeaway. SAP/Warp's tested baseline formulation produces low sub-Coulomb drift without aggressive anti-slip tuning. MuJoCo exposes stronger native controls for trading compliant friction regularization against drift suppression.

3.2 Kinetic friction

For ideal Coulomb sliding,

Ft = −μFn,    ρ = |Ft|/(μFn) ≈ 1

In the controlled free-sliding benchmark, SAP/Warp remained close to the friction-cone limit over the tested velocity range.

MuJoCo approached the same limit at higher sliding speed but showed stronger low-speed regularization. At the reference timestep,

ρSAP ≈ 0.99

throughout the tested range, whereas MuJoCo increased from approximately

0.89 → 0.99

as sliding speed increased.

A remaining open question is the transient increase in measured normal force after horizontal velocity initialization. This must be separated into true normal–tangential coupling, contact duty, and telemetry aggregation before drawing a stronger conclusion.

Takeaway. SAP/Warp more directly reproduces saturated Coulomb kinetic friction in the tested baseline; MuJoCo shows stronger velocity-dependent regularization near the sticking transition.

3.3 Force-matched simple pinch

Simple pinch provides the cleanest cross-engine friction test because the actual measured normal preload is matched instead of geometric compression.

For an ideal symmetric pinch:

r = 2μN/(mg)

The theoretical support threshold is:

r = 1

Both engines recover the expected transition:

r < 1 → slip     r > 1 → support

In the tested native configurations, SAP/Warp transitions closer to the ideal boundary:

rSAP ≈ 1.01–1.05

while MuJoCo requires approximately:

rMJ ≈ 1.18
Takeaway. Under measured-force-matched conditions, SAP/Warp's tested configuration reproduces the ideal Coulomb pinch-support threshold more closely. This is a configuration-level result, not a universal friction ranking.

4. Dynamic Contact

4.1 Impact and restitution

Both engines were independently calibrated to:

eeff = 0.8

at one reference impact condition.

After calibration, both transferred restitution very well across incident speed. Their timestep transfer differed substantially at the same absolute timestep: MuJoCo retained approximately the calibrated restitution, whereas SAP/Warp became increasingly dissipative as timestep increased.

However, the measured contact duration also differed substantially:

tc,MJ ≈ 3.3 tc,SAP

Therefore the same absolute timestep gives SAP/Warp significantly fewer integration steps per contact event.

MuJoCo preserves calibrated restitution over a wider absolute timestep range in the tested parameterizations, but a contact-timescale-matched experiment is still required before attributing the difference purely to timestep robustness.

A broader methodological lesson follows:

static stiffness matching ≠ dynamic contact-timescale matching

4.2 Repeated elastic impact

In repeated bouncing, SAP/Warp accumulated finite-step dissipation even with nominally non-dissipative material settings, while MuJoCo retained substantially more mechanical energy in the tested configuration.

This suggests particular caution for:

  • High-restitution collisions
  • Repeated impacts
  • Hopping and bouncing
  • Energy-sensitive control
  • Stiff contact with short contact duration

The result does not imply that SAP/Warp is physically incorrect. It shows that its finite-step compliant contact model can materially affect rebound and energy retention and therefore should be calibrated when those quantities matter.

5. Collision Representation and Contact Geometry

5.1 Analytic cavity: contact solver control

The analytic square cavity uses primitive box contacts and therefore provides a clean multi-contact insertion control.

Both engines successfully insert a centered peg.

For SAP/Warp, the initially failing 1-mm-offset case became stable after reducing rigid_gap from 1 mm to 0.1–0.5 mm.

The key lesson is that rigid_gap is not merely a numerical tolerance: when its scale approaches the physical clearance, early contact activation can change the trajectory.

Takeaway. SAP/Warp does not exhibit a generic multi-contact insertion failure. Contact activation relative to geometric clearance matters.

5.2 Round concave socket

The round socket exposes a different issue: collision representation.

Raw concave contact cannot be judged from final depth or reported penetration alone.

MuJoCo mesh-SDF produced many apparent insertions:

22/25

but none passed the project's strict physical screening. SAP raw/watertight mesh also produced invalid cavity witness behavior.

Using a shared 32-piece CoACD representation:

MuJoCo: 2/25 credible cases
SAP/Warp: 0/10 credible cases

under the currently tested parameter coverage.

MuJoCo received a substantially broader contact-parameter search, so this is not yet an engine-best comparison.

Takeaway. Tight manipulation can be limited more by collision representation and contact activation than by the contact solver itself.

5.3 Broad concave settling

The conclusion changes for less constrained geometry.

With the same CoACD dinner-plate representation, SAP/Warp and MuJoCo produced very similar sphere-in-plate equilibria.

Both also settled a three-plate stack without escape, although the resulting contact manifolds and lateral equilibria differed.

Takeaway. Broad concave settling is handled consistently by both engines; tight-clearance insertion is the harder representation stress test.

6. Manipulation: Panda Pick and Transfer

The Panda benchmark intentionally compares engine-native task behavior rather than forcing identical controller implementations.

The robot model, object, trajectory intent, finger effort cap, and task-success criteria are shared, while each engine uses its native controller/contact stack.

Task-level interpretation

This benchmark gives an important result:

microbenchmark superiority does not directly predict manipulation robustness

SAP/Warp exhibits

  • Larger mass/friction feasible region
  • Stronger long-hold retention
  • More continuous contact behavior

MuJoCo exhibits

  • Tighter short-horizon relative pose when successful
  • Better pose-perturbation robustness
  • Better transfer-rate robustness in the tested sweep

Neither description can be reduced to a single task-level winner.

8. What This Means for Robotics

Where SAP/Warp currently looks attractive

  • Physically interpretable compliant-contact parameters
  • Coulomb-like friction near the sticking/sliding transition
  • Continuous contact during manipulation
  • GPU-oriented large-scale simulation
  • System identification or domain randomization based on meaningful physical parameters

Where MuJoCo currently looks safer

  • Near-rigid high-stiffness static contact
  • Stable multi-contact manifolds
  • Energetic impact with coarse timesteps
  • Strong native anti-slip controls
  • Tolerance to geometric/pose perturbation in the tested manipulation setup

9. Final Conclusion

The experiments do not support a simple statement that either SAP/Warp or MuJoCo is universally more accurate.

Instead, they reveal different modeling tradeoffs.

SAP/Warp provides more directly interpretable compliant-contact parameters and, in several controlled friction benchmarks, behavior closer to ideal Coulomb contact. Its current limitations appear most clearly when contact becomes very stiff or collision geometry becomes tight and representation-sensitive.

MuJoCo provides a highly tunable soft-constraint formulation with strong near-rigid stability, effective anti-slip controls, and robust dynamic behavior in the tested impact regimes, but its parameters are less directly transferable as physical material properties.

Contact-law fidelity ≠ task-level performance.

Collision representation and contact manifold can dominate nominal solver differences.

For embodied robotics, simulator selection should therefore be based on the task-relevant observable and failure mode, rather than nominally matched contact parameters or a single global accuracy score.
