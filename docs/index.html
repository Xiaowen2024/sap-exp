SAP/Warp / Benchmarking / Cross-simulator study

Research report · September 2026

SAP/Warp vs MuJoCo:
contact-rich simulation benchmark

A controlled comparison of contact law, time stepping, collision representation, frictional behavior, and manipulation robustness across more than 650 experiments.

Scope
Rigid-body robotics simulation
Primary question
Which failure modes appear under which contact regimes?
Comparison basis
Observable behavior, not parameter-name equivalence

1. Executive summary

Bottom line

There is no universal winner. MuJoCo was the more consistent reference in the tested near-rigid, impact, and static-equilibrium regimes. SAP/Warp exposed useful material-like contact controls and tracked Coulomb friction more closely in several controlled compliant-contact tests. The practical result depends on the contact regime, collision representation, and task-level configuration.

FindingEvidence from the tested configurationsMeaning
Near-rigid contact MuJoCo maintained stable multi-contact stacks and near-zero static force-balance error. SAP/Warp showed contact-manifold switching and jitter at high pair stiffness. Increasing stiffness is not, by itself, a reliable path to higher fidelity.
Compliant friction SAP/Warp stayed closer to the Coulomb limit in free sliding and reached the ideal breakaway threshold more closely in the stable ramp and pinch tests. SAP/Warp is promising when an interpretable compliant friction response is the target, subject to normal-contact stability.
Impact and energy After calibration, MuJoCo transferred restitution more consistently across timesteps. In the elastic-bounce test, MuJoCo retained approximately the initial energy while SAP/Warp accumulated measurable dissipation. MuJoCo was the safer baseline for dynamic contact and restitution in these tests.
Geometry and robotics Broad concave settling was similar, but tight tilted insertion was sensitive to collision representation. In Panda tasks, feasible-region size and post-contact drift favored different engines. Collision geometry and the controller–contact interaction can dominate the apparent simulator ranking.

2. Scope and protocol

The study evaluates two rigid-body simulation stacks under controlled analytical, contact, and robotics tasks. Each benchmark is interpreted through physically meaningful observables: force balance, penetration, energy, velocity error, slip, pose drift, and task success.

Evaluation layerRepresentative testsPrimary observables
Contact lawSphere–plane stiffness; four-point box–plane calibrationForce–deformation slope, penetration, static force balance
Time steppingThin discs; dynamic impact; elastic bounceStability, restitution transfer, energy retention, settling
FrictionFree sliding; three-box ramp; simple pinchDeceleration, Coulomb utilization, breakaway threshold, creep
Collision representationAnalytic cavity; CoACD round socket; concave plateValid insertion, geometric clearance, final equilibrium
Task robustnessPanda pick-and-transferFeasible mass/friction region, pose success, transfer, hold drift

What was held fixed

  • Scene geometry, mass, gravity, control sequence, and evaluation windows whenever the task allowed it.
  • Time step and contact parameters were swept within each simulator's native parameterization.
  • Analytical references were used where available instead of treating either engine as ground truth.

What was not assumed

  • ke/tau and solref/solimp are not one-to-one equivalents.
  • A smaller penetration or a successful render is not sufficient evidence of valid concave geometry.
  • CPU MuJoCo wall time and CUDA SAP/Warp wall time are not a fair raw runtime ranking.

Interpretation rule. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, every result below describes the tested configuration and regime, not an engine-best claim.

3. Benchmark results

The experiments form a progression from contact-law behavior to task-level robustness. The main diagnostic is not a single score; it is the failure mode that appears when the operating regime changes.

3.1 Calibration and settling

Four-point box–plane interface

This calibration isolates the mapping between a requested pair stiffness and the aggregate stiffness measured through four simultaneous contacts.

Thin discs and layered box stacks

Thin-disc sweeps identified stable operating bands in both engines rather than a monotonic “harder is better” trend. In the calibrated layered-stack study, MuJoCo retained four internal contacts and stayed close to static force balance. SAP/Warp was stable at low stiffness, while high-stiffness cases developed contact switching and vertical jitter.

3.2 Motion and impact

25-ball rolling

The rolling benchmark compares tangential impulse allocation against an analytical pure-rolling reference. MuJoCo's standard tested configuration produced a velocity MSE of 0.00693; the best tested SAP/Warp configuration produced 0.347. This is evidence about the current configurations and sweep breadth, not a universal statement about all possible settings.

Result MuJoCo was closer to the selected ideal rolling reference. SAP/Warp's tangential impulse distribution differed systematically, showing that the friction law and solver response—not only the nominal μ—matter.

Dynamic impact and restitution

Both engines were calibrated to approximately e = 0.8 at the reference condition. When the time step increased to 0.5 ms, MuJoCo produced e = 0.803, whereas SAP/Warp produced e = 0.745. Across the matched-timescale extension, MuJoCo changed by approximately 0.003 in restitution while SAP/Warp changed by approximately 0.057.

Result MuJoCo transferred the calibrated impact response more robustly across the tested timesteps.

Elastic bouncing

In a 49-ball, 20-second bounce with a nominally elastic setting, MuJoCo retained approximately E/E0 ≈ 1.00. The best tested SAP/Warp configuration reached E/E0 = 0.834, indicating accumulated finite-step contact dissipation.

For SAP/Warp, ke, τ, and dt materially changed rebound height and energy retention. The result is a useful diagnostic of compliant-contact dissipation, not a claim that SAP/Warp cannot model energetic impacts.

3.3 Frictional contact

Free sliding

For a 1 kg cube sliding at v0 = 0.5 m/s, SAP/Warp had a 0.20% deceleration error and 0.54% stopping-distance error. MuJoCo had a 4.89% deceleration error and 0.25% stopping-distance error. The different metric ordering is important: “closer” depends on the observable being evaluated.

Three-box friction ramp and force hold

Only the SAP/Warp kpair = 104 N/m case passed the normal-stability gate; higher stiffness introduced contact switching before the friction test. Under the stable ramp, breakaway occurred at 4.528 N for SAP/Warp and 3.940 N for MuJoCo, against the ideal 4.905 N. SAP/Warp therefore showed less pre-slip in this matched setup.

Simple pinch

Both engines recovered the expected support transition near r = 2μN/(mg) = 1. SAP/Warp transitioned at approximately r = 0.959–1.010, while MuJoCo transitioned at r = 1.031–1.176 under the tested native configurations.

Result SAP/Warp was closer to the ideal Coulomb threshold in this force-matched pinch. MuJoCo's anti-slip and friction-specific solver controls remain important when comparing practical grasp retention.

3.4 Collision geometry and insertion

This benchmark separates collision representation from contact-law behavior. The same simulator can appear successful or unsuccessful depending on whether the geometry is analytic, convex-decomposed, raw mesh, or SDF-based.

Geometry testObserved resultInterpretation
Analytic square cavityBoth engines completed insertion under the tested clearance and tilt conditions.The basic insertion pipeline is not intrinsically simulator-specific.
Broad concave settlingSphere final height/radius: MuJoCo 27.238 / 33.524 mm; SAP/Warp 27.444 / 33.667 mm. Three plates preserved their order in both engines.Both engines can produce similar macroscopic equilibrium in a permissive concave contact.
Tight tilted round socketShared 32-piece CoACD: MuJoCo had 7/25 apparent entries and 2/25 credible cases; current SAP/Warp native sweep had 0/10 entries and 0/10 credible cases.Tight insertion exposed a collision-manifold failure mode in the tested SAP/Warp configuration.

Credibility rule. An apparent entry based only on small penetration or a raw concave mesh is not counted as a valid insertion. Pose, clearance, penetration relative to clearance, contact capacity, and collision representation must be checked together.

5. Interpretation and limitations

How to read the results

  1. Contact law: Is the force, friction, or restitution response physically appropriate?
  2. Parameter transfer: Does calibration at one timestep or load transfer to another?
  3. Geometry and manifold: Are contacts generated from a valid and sufficiently resolved representation?
  4. Task robustness: Does the full controller–contact system complete the intended task?

Limits of the current comparison

  • The native parameter sweeps are not symmetric in every experiment, especially for the round socket.
  • Several results compare different contact formulations by matching observable behavior, not identical equations.
  • Single-world CPU and CUDA wall times should not be used as a performance ranking.
  • Without physical ground truth, an equilibrium difference is a diagnostic, not proof that one engine is correct.
  • SAP/Warp and MuJoCo are both affected by collision representation, timestep, solver controls, and floating-point behavior.

Conclusion

MuJoCo is currently the more dependable reference for near-rigid equilibrium, impact transfer, and broad task reproducibility in the tested regimes. SAP/Warp offers a useful alternative when compliant, parameterized, GPU-oriented contact is the target, but its operating band must be established explicitly. The most informative next comparison is therefore not “which engine is more accurate?” but “which contact regime and failure mode does each formulation support reliably?”
