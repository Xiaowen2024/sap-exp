01 · EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

No universal winner—different contact regimes expose different strengths.

The comparison is strongest when it asks a physically explicit question: does a trajectory match an analytical reference, does a force balance close, does a contact remain stable, or does a robot task succeed?

Main conclusion

MuJoCo is the more consistent reference in the tested near-rigid and impact regimes.

It preserved four-point static contact in the calibrated stacks, transferred a calibrated restitution more robustly across timestep, and retained substantially more mechanical energy in repeated elastic bouncing.

Rolling MSE0.00693standard MuJoCovs.0.34721best tested SAP
SAP/Warp signal

Material-like pair stiffness and compliant friction are useful—but configuration-sensitive.

SAP reproduced the measured four-contact force–deformation slope and, in a stable friction ramp, reached the Coulomb breakaway threshold more closely than the tested MuJoCo configuration.

Most important caveat

High stiffness does not automatically mean higher fidelity.

SAP high-stiffness stacks developed contact-manifold switching and jitter; MuJoCo’s scalar stiffness mapping did not transfer unchanged from ground to dynamic box–box interfaces.

Robotics reading

Task outcomes are controller–contact interactions.

At frozen native Panda settings, SAP had the larger observed mass/friction feasible region, while MuJoCo showed lower hand-relative drift after contact was established. This is task-level evidence, not solver-equivalence evidence.

02 · HOW TO READ THE COMPARISON

Separate physical behavior from numerical bookkeeping.

The benchmark uses two complementary protocols: frozen native configurations to characterize each engine as used, and observable-matched experiments to remove a specific confound such as normal force or contact timescale.

A

Analytical or invariant references

For single-contact or low-complexity systems, compare trajectory, restitution, frictional acceleration, impulse, energy, or force balance with a known reference.

trajectoryimpulseenergyforce balance
B

Observable matching

Do not equate parameter names numerically. Match the measured interface slope, normal preload, or dynamic contact time, then compare the task outcome.

C

Task-level diagnostics

For stacks and robots without a unique ground truth, report contact duty, point count, penetration, slip, drift, settling, failure mode, and success gates together.

Parameter semantics

Functionally related does not mean mathematically equivalent.

solref/solimp and ke/tau both affect contact response, but the mapping depends on mass, geometry, active contacts, and timestep.

03 · EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE

Eight experiments, one consistent story.

Use the filters to read the report by physical phenomenon. Every result below is labeled by its comparison scope so that a native-model observation is not mistaken for a universal engine ranking.

01
Calibrationquasi-static normal contact

Four-point box–plane interface

Can a simple force–deformation law be measured and transferred between engines?

For a 40 mm cube constrained to vertical motion, the experiment directly measured the aggregate interface slope K = dFN/dδ under four known loads.

Result. SAP reproduced K ≈ 4kpair to better than 0.002% in the symmetric four-contact setup. MuJoCo was empirically calibrated to the same 4 × 106 N/m aggregate slope with a direct-format solref stiffness of 105 s−2.

MeaningBoth engines can be calibrated at one interface.The mapping is geometry-specific and does not automatically transfer to rotating or dynamic box–box contact.

02
Stackingthin discs + layered boxes

Settling, stiffness, and contact-manifold stability

Does increasing contact stiffness make a multi-contact stack more physically reliable?

The thin-disc native sweeps found usable operating bands in both engines. MuJoCo’s tested balanced point was dt = .25 ms, solref = 1 ms; SAP’s was ke = 105 N/m, τ = 3 ms, dt = .25–.5 ms.

In the calibrated layered-box study, both engines matched mean static force balance. MuJoCo retained four contacts at every internal interface. SAP was stable at low stiffness, but high-stiffness cases showed contact switching and jitter; its constraint coordinate also carried a repeatable ≈0.1 mm offset from raw geometric overlap.

Key lessonLower peak penetration is not enough.Contact duty, point count, tail velocity, and geometric reference must be reported with penetration.

03
Analytical dynamics25-ball rolling

Multi-contact tangential impulse allocation

When many contacts must preserve symmetric pure rolling, how closely does the trajectory follow the analytical reference?

This benchmark applies a horizontal force to a plate carrying 25 balls and compares world-frame velocities with the SimBenchmark analytical solution.

Result. MuJoCo reproduced the published standard value, MSE = 0.00692691 (m/s)²; its best tested sweep point reached 0.001146. The best tested SAP Drake configuration reached 0.347212.

Velocity MSE · lower is closer

The comparison is against this specific ideal pure-rolling reference, not all real contact behavior.

↗

04
Impactrestitution + long-horizon energy

Dynamic timestep transfer

After matching restitution and contact timescale at one reference impact, which response transfers more robustly as dt grows?

For a frictionless sphere–plane impact, both engines were calibrated to e = 0.8 and tc ≈ 30 ms at the reference case, then evaluated over incident speeds and timesteps.

At dt = .5 ms, v = .5 m/s, MuJoCo measured e = .8027, while SAP measured e = .7452. In a separate 20 s elastic-bounce test, the best tested SAP run retained E/E0 = .834, compared with ≈1.002 for MuJoCo.

Matched-timescale restitution · dt = .5 ms

Both are calibrated at the reference point; the difference is timestep transfer.

05
Frictionsliding, ramp, and pinch

Friction is a contact-state problem, not only a μ problem

Do the engines produce the same stick/slip behavior when the friction coefficient and normal load are controlled?

In free sliding, SAP stayed closer to the Coulomb deceleration limit over the tested speed range, while MuJoCo showed stronger low-speed regularization under the matched baseline. In the three-box ramp, only SAP kpair = 104 N/m passed the normal-stability gate; higher stiffness introduced contact switching before friction was tested.

In the force-matched simple pinch, both engines recovered the expected r = 2μN/(mg) = 1 threshold. SAP’s transition interval was r = .959–1.010, versus MuJoCo’s 1.031–1.176 under the tested native configurations.

Task-level resultSAP was closer to the ideal breakaway threshold in the stable ramp.Native MuJoCo anti-slip options changed the result, so this is not a universal friction ranking.

06
Geometrypeg-in-hole + concave plate

Collision representation can dominate the outcome

Is an apparent insertion or penetration actually a physically credible contact configuration?

Analytic square-cavity controls were successful in both engines. SAP’s 1 mm rigid_gap failed at a 1 mm offset because it changed contact activation; reducing it to 0.1–0.5 mm restored the offset insertion.

For the tilted round socket using the shared 32-piece CoACD representation, MuJoCo produced 2 credible cases out of 25; the tested SAP native configuration produced 0 out of 10. Raw mesh/SDF “entries” were rejected by the screening rule because they could contain invalid internal witnesses or excessive overlap.

For the shared concave dinner-plate representation, both engines settled the sphere and preserved the three-plate order, but reached different equilibria.

Interpretation boundaryDo not rank engines from raw mesh entry alone.This section compares tested collision representations and parameter coverage, not engine-best versus engine-best.

07
RoboticsPanda pick-and-transfer

Task robustness changes the practical ranking

Under a shared Panda task intent and a frozen native configuration, which engine preserves a grasp across mass, friction, pose, and transfer changes?

The corrected benchmark uses hand-relative pose, a common 15 N per-finger cap, bilateral-contact duty, drop, slip, rotation, and overlap gates. Controllers and timestep remain native by design, so this is an end-to-end task robustness study.

Mass/friction sweep. SAP passed 32/46 conditions; MuJoCo passed 4/46. Pose sweep. MuJoCo passed 14/19; SAP passed 10/19. Transfer rate. MuJoCo passed 7/7; SAP passed 6/7. During a 10 s final hold, MuJoCo’s corrected configuration accumulated ≈0.465 mm/s drift and failed the 5 mm gate; SAP remained within the gate but sat closer to the rotation limit.

08
Parameter behaviornative sweeps

Both engines have useful operating bands—not monotonic “harder is better” laws

What does parameter sensitivity reveal about the simulators themselves?

MuJoCo thin-disc sweeps showed that the dt/solref ratio, damping ratio, impedance shape, integrator, and solver interact non-monotonically. SAP sweeps showed that ke, τ, rigid_gap, and contact preset jointly determine penetration, settling, and geometric bias.

Across both engines, a parameter can improve one metric while damaging another: stiffer contact may reduce peak penetration but increase jitter, contact switching, or energy error. The practical output is a validated operating band for a task, not a single universal setting.

BOTTOM LINE

The right comparison is not “which solver wins?”

It is: which contact formulation, parameter regime, collision representation, and timestep produce the behavior the downstream task requires? On that question, the benchmark gives a useful division of labor: MuJoCo is the stronger current reference for several near-rigid and impact-sensitive tests; SAP/Warp shows meaningful advantages in observable compliant-contact semantics and selected task-level friction/robot regimes, while needing more care around high-stiffness multi-contact and concave insertion.
