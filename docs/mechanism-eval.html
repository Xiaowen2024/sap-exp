Research question. Instead of judging simulators by uncalibrated defaults or brute-force parameter sweeps, how do we evaluate whether an engine responds monotonically to first-principles tuning, unlocks native optimization mechanisms with a modest evaluation budget (B ≤ 5), and retains stability in a wide parameter plateau?

1. Why a New Evaluation Methodology?

The limitations of Evaluation Paradigm 1 (Default vs. Brute-Force Sweep)

Existing simulator benchmarks generally follow one of two extremes:

  • Extreme A: Out-of-the-Box Zero-Shot Default (B = 1). Simulators are run with factory default settings. While reproducible, this paradigm unfairly penalizes engines with intentionally compliant/safe default choices (e.g. SAP Warp's default soft contact ke=2500 N/m, which sags under dynamic tilting) and completely ignores dedicated native optimization mechanisms designed specifically for that contact regime (e.g. MuJoCo's noslip_iterations, impratio, or <pair solreffriction>).
  • Extreme B: Brute-Force Grid Sweep / Oracle Best (B ≥ 100). Simulators are swept over hundreds of parameter combinations to find the global minimum error. While this demonstrates peak potential, it is highly labor-intensive and misleading: an engine might find an accidental knife-edge optimum through non-physical parameter compensation (e.g., compensating for missing static friction using an anisotropic 4-sided pyramid cone artifact), yet be practically unusable because human engineers cannot reach that optimum without exhausting hundreds of trials.

Core Thesis. A fair, unified simulator evaluation should not force artificial parameter-name matching, nor should it reward accidental brute-force optima. It must evaluate how a simulator responds to first-principles physical mechanisms under a strict, realistic calibration budget (B ≤ 5).

2. The Unified 2D Evaluation Framework

2.1 The 2D evaluation matrix: Ground truth vs. Diagnostic first-principles

Traditional simulator benchmarking assumes that high-precision real-world mocap or sensor data is always available. However, in robotic manipulation and sim-to-real transfer, real datasets are expensive, sparse, or noisy. A complete simulator evaluation framework must systematically assess engines in both regimes:

Evaluation Dimension With Real-World Reference (有真实参考) Without Real Reference (First-Principles & Diagnostics)
1. Fidelity (保真度) • Task transfer & trajectory tracking (mocap nRMSE)
• Slip / breakaway / impact failure agreement		 • Analytic conservation laws & MDP dissipation
• Signorini / contact complementarity consistency diagnostics
2. Generalization (泛化性) • Zero-shot transfer to held-out real scenes / materials / disturbances • Mechanism coverage across physical regimes
• Parameter controllability & configuration portability
3. Usability (可用性与可控性) • Real-to-sim calibration budget (B ≤ 5 runs)
• Iteration cost & sensor dependency		 • Parameter interpretability (physical units vs. tuning knobs)
• Mechanism-aware tuning predictability (monotonicity |ρs|)

First-Principles Bridge. Mechanism-aware evaluation serves as the structural bridge across this 2D matrix:

$$\text{Physical Mechanism} \longrightarrow \text{Expected Behavior} \longrightarrow \text{Native Controls} \longrightarrow \text{Observable Diagnostics} \longrightarrow \text{Real-World Validation}$$

It does not pretend that simulators can be proven absolutely true without real data; rather, it guarantees that a simulator is physically consistent, monotonic, and controllable before spending expensive real-robot trials.

2.2 Three structured calibration stages

For every atomic physical regime, we evaluate simulators along three progressive stages:

  1. Stage 1 — Out-of-the-Box Default (B = 1): Factory default configuration without any task-specific tuning. Measures baseline usability.
  2. Stage 2 — First-Principles Mechanism-Guided Calibration (B ≤ 5): The user diagnoses the governing physical mechanism from theory (e.g., normal support bandwidth, tangential regularization decoupling, or impact restitution damping), sets an analytical prior, and performs at most 3 one-dimensional bisection/gradient steps along that single physical knob.
  3. Stage 3 — Brute-Force Grid Oracle (B ≈ 100): Exhaustive grid search over all available solver parameters. Used solely as a theoretical benchmark to compute small-budget regret:
    $$\text{Regret}_{B \le 5} = \text{nRMSE}_{B \le 5} - \text{nRMSE}_{\text{oracle}}$$

2.3 The three quantitative evaluation axes

Rather than conflating different aspects of simulator quality into a single score, we evaluate three orthogonal axes:

Evaluation Axis Quantified Metrics Physical & Practical Meaning
Axis 1: Physical Fidelity • nRMSE(B=1)
• nRMSE(B≤5)
• nRMSE(Oracle)		 Trajectory and observable accuracy against ground-truth real mocap or analytical reference across calibration stages.
Axis 2: Calibratability & Efficiency • Budget-to-pass Bpass ≤ 1.0

Axis 3: Predictability & Robustness • Spearman monotonicity |ρs|

3. Experimental Results on 4 Physical Regimes

3.1 Regime I: Inclined sliding & dynamic tilting (Slope Slider task-2 wood & task-3 metal)

First-principles mechanism: Normal contact compliance bandwidth (τn, kn) relative to external tilting rate (180 Hz). On dynamically tilting inclines, normal contact must be stiff enough to prevent gravity sag and respond faster than the tilting rate (τn ≤ 5 ms).

3D Task Setup: Dynamically Tilting Plate & Free-Sliding Block

  • Three-Dimensional Spatial Geometry: A rectangular rigid board (30 cm × 20 cm × 2 cm) is mounted in 3D space. A solid 3D test cube (wood or metal, mass = 339.7 g, 5 cm edge) rests freely on the plate surface under gravity without any artificial rail or kinematic constraint.
  • Dynamic 3D Kinematics (180 Hz): Rather than resting at a static inclination, the plate undergoes high-speed 3D pitching and angular acceleration up to ∼30°, driven by dynamic robot motions and tracked by optical motion capture at 180 Hz (Δt = 5.56 ms). This high-frequency rotation creates continuously changing 3D normal forces N(t) and multi-axis tangential shear forces.
  • 3D Stick-Slip Friction Transition: The block is governed by 3D Coulomb friction at the plate-block contact interface. When the 3D inertial and gravity force vectors exceed the static friction cone limit, the block transitions from sticking to 3D sliding.
  • Sim-to-Real Prediction Target: Simulators replay the identical 6-DoF 3D rigid trajectory of the board, and must predict the unconstrained 3D motion trajectory [x(t), y(t), z(t)] of the sliding block. Accuracy is evaluated against the ground-truth 3D Mocap trajectory (reporting 3D position RMSE in mm and normalized nRMSE).
  • Why Task-3 (Metal) Is Extremely Demanding: task-2 wood has higher surface friction (μ ≈ 0.45) providing forgiving traction margin. In contrast, task-3 metal has low friction (μ ≈ 0.26) combined with heavy block mass (340 g). If normal contact compliance is too soft or reacts slower than the 180 Hz tilting motion (τn > 5.56 ms), 3D normal support lags behind, causing instantaneous friction collapse and runaway 3D drift (577.7 mm).
Engine & Setup Stage 1: Default (B = 1) Stage 2: 1st-Principles (B ≤ 5) Stage 3: Oracle (B ≈ 100) Monotonicity |ρs| ±2× Neighborhood Pass Rate (max)
MuJoCo (task-2 wood) 0.52 (9.4 mm) 0.44 (8.0 mm) 0.44 (8.0 mm) 0.88 16 / 16 (100%) (max=0.56)
SAP Warp (task-2 wood) 0.58 (10.4 mm) 0.36 (6.4 mm) 0.27 (4.9 mm) 0.83 16 / 16 (100%) (max=0.74)
MuJoCo (task-3 metal) 0.71 (27.0 mm) 0.19 (7.3 mm) 0.17 (6.5 mm) 1.00 15 / 16 (93.8%) (max=1.57)
SAP Warp (task-3 metal) ❌ 15.20 (577.7 mm) 0.15 (5.8 mm) 0.13 (5.1 mm) 1.00 16 / 16 (100%) (max=0.25)

Key Finding. SAP's soft literal default (ke=2500 N/m, τ=10 ms) fails on task-3 metal (B = 1), but its Calibration Efficiency and Predictability are exceptional: setting rigid-body compliance directly from first principles (drake: ke=105 N/m, τ=3 ms) in just 1 step (B = 2) drops error from 577.7 mm down to 7.49 mm (nRMSE = 0.20), forming a completely flat plateau where all 16 neighboring configurations satisfy nRMSE ≤ 0.25.

3.2 Regime II: Planar rotating friction & reversal (Turntable task-1 wood & task-3 metal)

First-principles mechanism: On a horizontal rotating plate, normal load is constant (mg). Accuracy is governed entirely by whether tangential friction tracking damping τt is decoupled from normal compliance (kn, τn).

Engine & Decoupling Mechanism Stage 1: Default (B = 1) Stage 2: 1st-Principles (B ≤ 5) Stage 3: Oracle (B ≈ 100) Monotonicity |ρs| ±2× Neighborhood Pass Rate (max)
MuJoCo (Default coupled pyramidal) 1.29 (17.2 mm) 0.77 (10.2 mm) 0.73 (9.7 mm) 1.00 (jitter at low dr) ❌ 9 / 16 (56.2%) (max=2.69)
MuJoCo (Unlocked solreffriction) — 0.77 (10.2 mm) 0.73 (9.7 mm) 1.00 (decoupled) 16 / 16 (100%) (max=0.83)
SAP Warp (Native decoupled Rt = σ wi) 1.13 (15.0 mm) 0.85 (11.2 mm) 0.82 (10.8 mm) 1.00 (native) 16 / 16 (100%) (max=1.12)

Native vs. Configurable Decoupling. SAP Warp decouples tangential friction regularization Rt = σ wi natively in the solver, delivering 16 / 16 configuration robustness out of the box. MuJoCo couples normal and tangential directions by default (failing 7 of 16 neighborhood configs), but achieves identical 16 / 16 robustness once the user unlocks its native mechanism (cone="elliptic" + <pair solreffriction="0.004 1.0"/>).

3.3 Regime III: High-speed centrifugal breakaway (Turntable task-2 metal, 6 rad/s)

First-principles diagnosis: Under 6 rad/s acceleration, the block undergoes high-speed centrifugal breakaway (vrel ∼ 1 m/s). Sweeping contact solver compliance (kn, τn) across 80–96 configurations is the wrong physical knob. Only calibrating high-speed kinetic friction μk directly addresses the physical mechanism:

Calibration Strategy Evaluation Budget B MuJoCo (cone="elliptic") SAP Warp (drake) Physical Interpretation
Strategy A: Blind Contact Compliance Sweep (ke, tau / solref) B = 80–96 runs ❌ 5.38 (31.0 mm) ❌ 4.65 (26.8 mm) Wrong mechanism: contact stiffness does not govern breakaway slip distance.
Strategy B: Relying on 4-sided Pyramid Artifact (cone="pyramidal") B = 1–40 runs 2.36 → 1.26 (7.25 mm) — Non-isotropic √2μ diagonal force accidentally mimics higher friction.
Strategy C: First-Principles Kinetic Friction Calibration (μk → 0.230) Only B = 4 runs 0.80 (4.61 mm) 0.78 (4.48 mm) Strictly monotonic (|ρs| = 1.00). Both engines match reality within 4.6 mm.

4. Evaluation Paradigm 1 vs. Paradigm 2 Findings

Comparing the conclusions drawn under traditional evaluation vs. our mechanism-aware framework highlights why evaluation methodology matters:

Evaluation Dimension Evaluation Paradigm 1 (Traditional: Default & Brute-Force Sweep) Evaluation Paradigm 2 (Mechanism-Aware & Calibration-Budgeted)
Slope Slider task-3 Concludes SAP Warp is broken out of the box (nRMSE = 15.20) while MuJoCo is accurate (0.71). Reveals that setting the rigid-body preset drake (B = 2) restores SAP Warp to 0.20, and SAP Warp possesses a significantly wider, flatter plateau (16 / 16 vs 15 / 16).
Turntable task-2 breakaway Concludes MuJoCo is superior (nRMSE = 1.26–2.36 vs SAP 4.65–5.75) across 100-run sweeps. Proves that both engines fail when solving true isotropic Coulomb friction, and MuJoCo only won due to a 4-sided pyramid cone artifact (√2μ). Calibrating kinetic friction directly (B = 4) brings both to 0.78–0.80.
Turntable rotating friction Reports comparable tuned numbers (0.73 vs 0.82), obscuring stability differences. Reveals that SAP decouples tangential friction natively (16 / 16 robustness), while MuJoCo couples them by default (9 / 16) and requires explicit solreffriction unlocking to match SAP.
Dynamic Impact Restitution Reports that both engines under-bounce at default and MuJoCo tunes closer to target apex height. Reveals the fundamental trade-off: MuJoCo achieves higher restitution (e = 0.73) via knife-edge under-damping that risks energy explosion (e > 1.0), while SAP Warp guarantees unconditional energy stability (e < 1.0).
